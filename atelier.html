The Atelier

Every sprite in Emberveil is drawn here, on the SNES's own colour grid, and shipped at 1×. This page is the receipt.

01

Five-bit

The SNES stored colour as BGR555 — five bits a channel, thirty-two levels. So every channel of every pixel we ship is a multiple of eight, and white is #F8F8F8, never #FFFFFF. The numbers below are measured by decoding the PNGs back off disk, not by trusting the code that wrote them.

The quantiser

snes(v) = min(248, round(v / 8) × 8) — one function, applied to every colour before it is allowed onto a surface. The rasteriser throws on an off-grid value rather than rounding it quietly, which is why the off-grid count opposite is zero and not nearly zero.

02

Integer only

Fractional scaling of pixel art is the one unforgivable sin. Step the Slagmaw through the factors and watch the arithmetic stay whole — there is no ×2.5 on this bench because there is no ×2.5 in the game.

Rendered —

The encounter stage is 192 × 168 — 8:7, the real SNES aspect — at ×2, which is 384 CSS px inside a 390 px viewport with 3 px of gold bezel each side and nothing left over. On a DPR-3 phone that is 1152 device px: an exact ×3 of the CSS layer and ×6 of the stage. Every step stays whole.

03

The Slagfen

An ash basin under a bruised sky, where the old refinery stacks still burn. Painted per 8×8 tile the way the hardware spent its palette: the median tile here holds — colours.

Scene ramps

04

The adepts

16 × 24, exactly two tiles by three, about 2.7 heads tall. The head is a silhouette — hair or helmet — never a face; at this width the eyes are one dark pixel pair and nothing else. Action poses widen to 24 × 32 because an outstretched weapon needs the extra tile column. And the critical pose is a whole different drawing, not an icon hung on the idle: that is how the party's condition reads across the field with no UI element saying so.

05

The bestiary

Zero idle frames, by design. A monster built as one sprite with a rotating four-slot ramp is more alive than one with a mediocre four-frame loop and costs a quarter of the art. The Slagmaw's seams below are burning on exactly that trick — one drawing, four palette rotations.

06

The rail

Not a 1 px stroke: a 4 px lit metal rail with corners chamfered over four pixels on a 45° diagonal, lit from the upper left like everything else. The geometry is ported verbatim and the metal is swapped — silver out, our gold in. Behind it runs the interior wash: twenty steps, subtracting eight from all three channels each time and clamping at zero, which is what makes the foot of a window go deep instead of grey.

Interior wash — 20 steps

— —

The battle band paints steps — of it. Windows at different heights sample different parts of one continuous wash, which is why stacked windows look like a single lit surface.

07

The gauge

The single best piece of UI in the source material and the reason is its proportions: a 40 × 7 capsule whose fill is exactly three rows — grey, white, grey — so it reads as a rounded tube at three pixels tall. The empty track has no colour at all. The window's wash shows straight through it, so a gauge at the top of the band and one at the foot look different, and that is correct.

08

The face

Seven-pixel cap height in an eight-pixel cell, proportional rather than monospaced, and — the trait almost every retro-web attempt misses — two-pixel stems. Every glyph carries a hard 1 px black shadow at (+1, +1): no blur, no alpha, non-optional. It is what keeps white text legible over a painted battlefield.

09

Relics & effects

Sixteen-pixel icons and the strike effects. Spell and impact art is an overlay held for a handful of ticks, then gone — actions are translations, not animations.

Effects

10

The ledger

Every file in the library, measured off the shipped bytes. Cols is the palette count, budgeted at fifteen plus transparent per sprite. A painted scene is budgeted per 8×8 tile instead — that is how the hardware spent it — so those rows carry a Tile figure, median over max, and it is the max that has to stay under fifteen. Dith is 2×2 checkerboard coverage against a ceiling of four percent, because dithering is a spice and not a technique. The build fails on a breach rather than reporting one.
